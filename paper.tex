\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This paper examines how information about relative performance affects
distributive preferences and beliefs about the role of effort versus luck in
generating inequality.
Using an online experiment, participants complete a code-recognition task and
are randomly assigned to one of three feedback conditions: no feedback, told
they performed \emph{above} a randomly matched peer, or told they performed
\emph{below} a randomly matched peer.
They then act as impartial spectators and distribute a fixed sum between two
workers whose inequality arose from either differential performance (merit
scenario) or a lottery (luck scenario).
Participants told they outperformed their peer redistribute approximately
\$0.59 less to the lower-earning worker in the merit scenario compared to the
no-feedback baseline --- a 24 percent reduction from the mean --- and score
0.78 points higher on an effort-attribution belief scale (0--10).
No significant effect is found for participants told they underperformed, and
the luck scenario serves as a placebo with no treatment effects.
These results are consistent with self-serving attribution: learning that
one outperformed a peer reinforces beliefs that inequality reflects effort,
which in turn reduces redistribution demand in merit-relevant contexts.
\end{abstract}

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} redistribution, relative performance feedback,
self-serving attribution, fairness, online experiment\\
\noindent\textbf{JEL codes:} C91, D31, D63, D91

\section{Introduction}

Free-market economies inherently generate unequal outcomes, and while modern
democratic societies rely on redistribution to mitigate these disparities,
there is persistent disagreement over which inequalities are considered
legitimate \citep{AlesinGiulian2011, Piketty2014}.
A central dimension of this debate concerns the \emph{source} of inequality:
outcomes attributed to effort are typically viewed as more deserving than those
arising from luck.
This intuition is formalised in the accountability framework, which models
redistributive preferences as judgments about deservingness and predicts lower
redistribution when inequality reflects effort rather than chance
\citep{Konow2000, Cappelen2007}.
Underlying this framework is a well-documented empirical regularity: beliefs
about income determinants drive redistribution preferences
\citep{Piketty1995, Alesina2005, Benabou2006}.
People who believe inequality arises from effort tend to favour less
redistribution; those who attribute it to luck or circumstance tend to favour
more.

To isolate such normative judgments from material self-interest, studies
often employ impartial spectator designs in which allocators distribute income
between others and receive no payoffs themselves \citep{Almas2020}.
While this approach successfully removes monetary incentives, it does not imply
psychological neutrality.
A growing body of evidence shows that recent experiences of success or failure
can shape how individuals evaluate deservingness, even when they subsequently
make allocation decisions on behalf of others
\citep{Cassar2019, Deffains2016}.
In this sense, impartiality is material rather than cognitive, and spectators’
own experiences may influence how they apply the accountability principle.

The stakes of understanding what shapes these beliefs extend to political
polarization.
\citet{Deffains2016} argue that the experience of success and failure is
itself a driver of redistributive polarization through the self-serving bias
(SSB): individuals who succeed at a task attribute their success to effort
and favour less redistribution, while those who fail attribute failure to
circumstance and shift toward more.
Because success and wealth are correlated, this mechanism creates a systematic
wedge between rich and poor in their perceived causes of inequality.
Wealthy individuals, as \citet{Deffains2016} put it, ``do not only oppose
redistribution because they expect to be net payers, but also because the SSB
systematically shifts their fairness principles.’’
The implication is stark: anything that alters an individual’s sense of
their own relative standing could reshape redistribution preferences --- not
through material self-interest, but through belief updating about whether
inequality reflects effort or luck.
A natural follow-up question is what shapes those beliefs.
Social comparisons are ubiquitous: workers learn their rank in a wage
distribution, students receive class rankings, and online platforms
continuously display relative performance metrics.
Does learning where one stands in a distribution affect how one interprets the
sources of inequality --- and through that channel, redistribution preferences?

This paper provides experimental evidence on this mechanism.
Participants complete a performance task and are randomly assigned to one of
three conditions: (i) \emph{no feedback} about relative standing; (ii) told
they performed \emph{above} a randomly matched peer (\emph{above} group);
or (iii) told they performed \emph{below} a matched peer (\emph{below}
group).
Subsequently, all participants act as impartial spectators and decide how to
distribute a fixed monetary sum between two workers, in two scenarios: one
where inequality arose from differential performance (merit), and one where it
arose from a lottery (luck).

The key findings are three.
First, participants told they outperformed a peer redistribute significantly
less in the merit scenario: \$1.87 on average versus \$2.47 in the
no-feedback group, a difference of \$0.59 that is statistically significant
at the 5 percent level in OLS regressions with and without controls.
Second, the same group scores approximately 0.78 points higher on an
effort-attribution scale (0--10), consistent with self-serving belief
updating, though this difference is only marginally significant individually
($p \approx 0.086$) while the joint test across groups is significant
($p = 0.028$, ANOVA).
Third, the effect is asymmetric: participants told they underperformed do not
significantly change their redistribution or belief scores relative to the
no-feedback baseline.
Taken together, these results suggest that positive performance signals
trigger self-serving attribution that translates into reduced redistributive
generosity --- but that negative signals do not produce a symmetric reverse
effect.

These results are consistent with the self-serving attribution account of
redistribution developed by \citet{Deffains2016}: individuals who perform well
relative to others update their beliefs about the role of effort in generating
inequality and become less redistributive.
Our design extends their framework by using \emph{randomly assigned comparative
feedback}, providing clean causal identification and isolating the relative
rather than absolute performance channel.
One notable difference is that \citet{Deffains2016} find SSB effects on both
sides --- success reduces redistribution and failure increases it --- whereas
our evidence is concentrated on the winning side.
The absence of a significant response among participants told they underperformed
suggests that the SSB in our setting is more asymmetric: learning one beat a
peer is enough to trigger meritocratic updating, but learning one lost does
not produce a symmetric shift toward redistribution.
The absence of any treatment effect in the luck scenario further supports
the attribution channel over non-specific mood or attention effects.

The paper most directly relates to \citet{Almas2020}, from whose spectator
design we adapt both the merit and luck redistribution tasks, and to
\citet{Cassar2019}, whose focus on how performance comparisons shape
distributive preferences motivates our research question.
Our contribution is to provide clean experimental identification of the
direction and asymmetry of this effect using randomly assigned relative
feedback.

Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews the related literature.
Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the experimental design.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:lit}

This paper contributes to three strands of literature.

\paragraph{Beliefs about inequality and redistribution.}
A foundational insight from \citet{Piketty1995} is that beliefs about social
mobility and the role of effort versus luck determine preferences for
redistribution in equilibrium.
\citet{Alesina2005} provide empirical evidence: Americans attribute poverty
more to individual failure than Europeans, which partly explains the
cross-country gap in redistribution.
\citet{Benabou2006} model how self-serving motivated reasoning sustains
two equilibria --- a ``belief in a just world'' and a more luck-oriented view
--- with different steady-state redistribution levels.
Our experiment directly tests the mechanism by which an informational shock
(relative performance feedback) updates beliefs and behaviour.

\paragraph{Impartial spectator and fairness views.}
\citet{Cappelen2007} and \citet{Cappelen2013} use spectator designs to
classify subjects into fairness types (libertarian, meritocratic, egalitarian)
and show that a majority of individuals accept some redistribution away from
luck-generated inequality even when indifferent to merit-generated inequality.
We adapt their two-scenario design (merit and luck) to test whether
third-party redistribution decisions are affected by information about the
spectator's own relative performance --- a test of whether the spectator role
is truly impartial.

\paragraph{Self-serving attribution and social comparisons.}
Self-serving attribution refers to the tendency to attribute successes
internally (effort) and failures externally (luck or circumstance)
\citep{Miller1975, Mezulis2004}.
In an economic context, \citet{Babcock1995} document that parties in
bargaining inflate self-assessments of a fair outcome in a self-serving
direction.
Closer to our design, \citet{Deffains2016} show that individuals who
experience unfair treatment become more supportive of redistribution.
\citet{Cassar2019} show that framing one's own performance as a failure
(relative to a counterfactual) shapes distributive preferences; our design
extends this insight by using randomly assigned comparative feedback,
allowing clean causal identification.
We add to this literature by focusing on the \emph{comparative} and
\emph{asymmetric} dimension: the same absolute performance level can produce
different attribution and redistribution depending on whether one learns one
stands above or below a peer, and the effect appears concentrated on the
winning side.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

The experiment was conducted online using JATOS and involved two distinct
roles: \emph{workers} and \emph{spectators}.

\textbf{Workers} ($n = 2$) completed the code-recognition task under real
monetary incentives.
They were told in advance that their earnings would depend on their relative
performance against each other, and that a third party (a spectator) would
subsequently decide whether and how much to redistribute between them.
Workers received real monetary payments determined by both their relative task
performance and a randomly selected spectator's redistribution decision.

\textbf{Spectators} ($n = 134$) were recruited through personal networks
(friends, family, and colleagues from two workplaces), completed the full
experiment online in approximately 7--10 minutes, and received no monetary
compensation.
Spectators were informed before making their redistribution decisions that the
workers were real individuals who had completed the same task and would
receive actual monetary payouts determined by one randomly selected
spectator's allocation.
The impartial-spectator design thus combines the removal of material
self-interest (spectators have no stake in the allocation) with real stakes
for the workers, ensuring that decisions carry genuine consequences.

\subsection{Part 1: Code-Recognition Task}

Participants completed a code-recognition task over five rounds of 45~seconds
each.
In each round, a target code was displayed at the top of the screen.
Participants were shown a grid of codes and had to check as many instances of
the target as possible.
Scoring: $+1$~point per correct identification, $-1$~point per false alarm,
$0$~points for a miss.
The maximum possible score was 200~points; sample means range from 91 to 97
points across treatment groups (Table~\ref{tab:balance}).

Before seeing their results, participants answered three pre-feedback
self-report questions:
(i) effort exerted (slider, 0--10);
(ii) guessed score (numeric, range $-200$ to $200$);
(iii) relative performance prediction (``I would perform better than or the
same as the other participant'' vs.\ ``I would perform worse'').

\subsection{Treatments: Relative Performance Feedback}
\label{sec:treatments}

After the self-report, participants were randomly assigned to one of three
feedback conditions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No feedback.} Participants were informed they could proceed to
    Part~2 without seeing their score or any comparison information.
  \item \textbf{Above.} Participants were shown their score and told:
    ``You performed above your randomly matched peer.''
  \item \textbf{Below.} Participants were shown their score and told:
    ``You performed below your randomly matched peer.''
\end{enumerate}

Each spectator in the relative-feedback group was matched to one of the two
workers and shown their own score together with whether they outperformed or
underperformed that specific worker.
The comparison was thus based on real task scores from a real counterpart,
making the feedback both credible and consequential: the worker a spectator
was compared to was also the worker whose payoff their redistribution decision
would partly determine.
Approximately half of the relative-feedback group was told they outperformed
their matched worker (above) and half that they underperformed (below).

\subsection{Part 2: Distribution Decisions}
\label{sec:distdecisions}

After the feedback screen, all participants acted as impartial spectators and
made two sequential redistribution decisions adapted from \citet{Almas2020}.

\subsubsection*{Decision 1 --- Merit Scenario}

Participants read that two real participants --- the two workers who had
previously completed the same task --- were identified as Worker~A and
Worker~B.
Worker~A had received a larger initial payment because they performed better;
Worker~B received less because they performed worse.
Participants were asked to decide how to redistribute a total endowment
between the two workers.
The decision was presented as a set of discrete options covering
the full range from keeping full inequality to reversing it, with an equal
split as the midpoint.
The primary outcome variable is the amount allocated to the lower-earning
Worker~B (\texttt{redist\_merit}, in USD).

\subsubsection*{Decision 2 --- Luck Scenario}

After completing a demographics survey (including the effort--luck belief
slider), participants saw a second decision.
The scenario was identical in structure but attributed the earnings difference
to a lottery with no regard for performance.
The primary outcome variable is the amount allocated to the lottery loser,
Worker~D (\texttt{redist\_luck}, in USD).

\subsection{Key Outcome and Mediator Variables}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Redistribution (merit):} USD allocated to Worker~B.
  \item \textbf{Redistribution (luck):} USD allocated to Worker~D.
  \item \textbf{Effort--luck belief (\texttt{effort\_luck}):}
    Recorded after the merit decision via the question: ``To what extent do
    you think the initial income of participants in the previous task was
    determined by effort versus luck?'' (slider: 0 = 100\% luck,
    10 = 100\% effort).
    This variable serves as the hypothesised mediator between feedback and
    redistribution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sample}

Spectators were recruited through the researcher's personal networks: friends,
family, and employees at two workplaces.
The analysis sample consists of $N = 134$ spectators after excluding
non-engagers (zero score and zero clicks) and statistical outliers.
This is a convenience sample and is therefore not nationally representative;
the main implication for external validity is that findings describe the
direction of the self-serving attribution effect under incentivized conditions
but may not generalise to other populations in terms of magnitudes.
Importantly, treatment assignment was random within this sample, so internal
validity is not compromised.

Table~\ref{tab:balance} presents descriptive statistics by treatment group.
Groups are balanced on pre-determined covariates: age ($p = 0.99$), gender
($p = 0.14$), task score ($p = 0.57$), and prior belief about relative
performance ($p = 0.43$), confirming that randomisation was successful.
The effort--luck belief score differs significantly across groups ($p = 0.028$),
which is itself a key outcome; importantly, this measure is elicited
\emph{after} the feedback treatment, so it cannot confound treatment
assignment.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Effort--Luck Beliefs}
\label{sec:beliefs}

Figure~\ref{fig:main} (left panel) shows mean effort--luck belief by treatment
group.
Participants told they outperformed a peer score markedly higher on the
effort-attribution scale ($\overline{EL}_{\text{above}} = 7.6$, SD = 2.3)
relative to the no-feedback group ($\overline{EL}_{\text{nofb}} = 6.8$, SD = 1.9)
and the below group ($\overline{EL}_{\text{below}} = 6.3$, SD = 2.6).
A one-way ANOVA rejects equality of group means at the 5 percent level
($F(2,128) = 3.78$, $p = 0.028$).

Table~\ref{tab:regressions} (columns 1--2) presents OLS regressions of
\texttt{effort\_luck} on treatment dummies, with no-feedback as the baseline.
Being told one outperformed a peer increases the effort-attribution score by
0.78 points (SE = 0.45, $p = 0.086$), while being told one underperformed
decreases it by 0.51 points (SE = 0.50, $p = 0.306$).
Neither pairwise coefficient reaches the 5 percent significance threshold,
but the pattern is consistent with a self-serving attribution mechanism:
the feedback most likely to be flattering (above) raises effort attribution,
while the feedback that implies underperformance (below) produces little
symmetric updating.
Controlling for age, gender, and task score leaves both coefficients
essentially unchanged (column 2), ruling out demographic confounding.
Three participants did not complete the effort--luck slider; the belief
regressions use $N = 131$.

\subsection{Redistribution in the Merit Scenario}
\label{sec:merit}

The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:main} shows mean redistribution in the
merit scenario.
The above group redistributes the least (\$1.87, SD = \$1.38), compared to
the no-feedback baseline (\$2.47, SD = \$1.18) and the below group
(\$2.39, SD = \$1.33).
The one-way ANOVA yields $p = 0.065$, marginally failing the conventional
threshold at the omnibus level.

Columns 3--4 of Table~\ref{tab:regressions} report the main OLS regressions.
Being told one outperformed reduces redistribution by \$0.59 (SE = \$0.27,
$p < 0.05$) relative to the no-feedback group, corresponding to a 24 percent
reduction from the no-feedback mean.
This estimate is robust to adding demographic and performance controls
(column 4: coefficient $-\$0.56$, SE = \$0.27, $p < 0.05$).
In contrast, the below coefficient is small ($-\$0.08$, SE = \$0.27,
$p = 0.77$) and statistically indistinguishable from zero,
confirming the asymmetric pattern visible in Figure~\ref{fig:main}.

These results establish a clear causal chain from relative feedback to
redistribution in the expected direction, but concentrated on the winning
side: participants who learn they outperformed a peer become measurably less
redistributive in a merit context, whereas participants who learn they
underperformed do not respond significantly.

\subsection{Redistribution in the Luck Scenario}
\label{sec:luck}

As a placebo, we examine redistribution in the luck scenario, where earnings
are explicitly attributed to a lottery and performance feedback is normatively
irrelevant.
If feedback affected redistribution through a non-specific channel --- such as
mood, engagement, or demand effects --- we would expect treatment differences
in both scenarios.
Table~\ref{tab:balance} shows that group means in the luck scenario are
\$3.16, \$2.70, and \$3.05 for the no-feedback, above, and below groups,
respectively, and the one-way ANOVA cannot reject equality ($p = 0.379$).
The null result in the luck scenario is consistent with the merit-redistribution
effect operating through performance-specific attribution beliefs rather than
a general mood or engagement channel.

\subsection{Heterogeneity by Surprise}
\label{sec:surprise}

To probe the attribution mechanism further, we classify participants in the
relative-feedback group by whether the feedback was surprising.
\emph{Positively surprised} participants predicted they would not outperform
their peer but were told they did.
\emph{Negatively surprised} participants predicted they would outperform but
were told they underperformed.

If self-serving attribution drives the result, the redistribution effect
should be sharpest among those for whom the positive signal was unexpected ---
unexpected confirmation of high standing is arguably more diagnostic and
self-affirming than expected confirmation.
Column~5 of Table~\ref{tab:regressions} reports OLS regressions of merit
redistribution on these surprise indicators, estimated within the
relative-feedback group ($N = 91$).
Relative to confirmed-outcome participants (those whose feedback matched their
prior expectation), negatively surprised participants redistribute \$0.53 more
(SE = \$0.30, $p = 0.082$), a marginally significant result in the expected
direction.
Positively surprised participants do not differ significantly from the baseline
(\$0.30, SE = \$0.77, $p = 0.70$).
We interpret the marginal result for negatively surprised participants as
suggestive that unexpected bad news about relative standing may trigger
compensatory redistribution, though the relative-group sample is underpowered
for this analysis and caution is warranted.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper provides experimental evidence that relative performance feedback
shapes both beliefs about the role of effort in generating inequality and
downstream redistribution preferences.
Participants told they outperformed a peer attribute more of the task
outcome to effort and redistribute \$0.59 less per round in a merit scenario
relative to a no-feedback baseline --- a 24 percent reduction.
The effect is asymmetric: underperformers do not significantly change either
beliefs or redistribution.
A luck scenario, where performance feedback is normatively irrelevant by
design, yields no treatment effects, suggesting the mechanism is specific to
merit-relevant attribution rather than a general engagement or mood effect.

Taken together, these findings support a self-serving attribution account of
redistribution preferences: receiving positive comparative feedback leads
individuals to update the belief that inequality reflects effort, and this
updated belief reduces their willingness to redistribute in settings where
merit is salient.
The asymmetry --- winners become less redistributive, losers do not become
more so --- mirrors the well-documented asymmetry in self-serving attribution
in psychology \citep{Mezulis2004} and has implications for the design of
performance information systems.

These findings have implications for the study of inequality and redistribution
in a world where performance comparisons are increasingly salient --- on
professional platforms, social networks, and in educational settings.
The self-serving updating documented here suggests that rising relative
visibility of performance rankings could systematically reduce support for
redistribution among those who ``win'' such comparisons, potentially
contributing to the polarisation of redistribution preferences.

Future work should (i) increase sample size to improve power, particularly
for the heterogeneity and mediation analyses; (ii) test whether the effect
persists when participants receive absolute score information alongside
relative feedback, to disentangle comparative and informational channels;
and (iii) conduct a formal mediation analysis to quantify the share of the
redistribution effect that operates through the effort-attribution belief
measure.
