\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This paper examines how information about relative performance affects
distributive preferences and beliefs about the role of effort versus luck in
generating inequality.
Using an online experiment, participants complete a code-recognition task and
are randomly assigned to one of three feedback conditions: no feedback, told
they performed \emph{above} a randomly matched peer, or told they performed
\emph{below} a randomly matched peer.
They then act as impartial spectators and distribute a fixed sum between two
workers whose inequality arose from either performance (merit scenario) or a
lottery (luck scenario).
Participants told they outperformed their peer redistribute approximately
\$0.60 less to the lower-earning worker in the merit scenario compared to the
no-feedback baseline, and score 0.8 points higher on an effort-attribution
belief scale (0--10).
These results are consistent with self-serving attribution: learning that
one outperformed a peer leads to stronger beliefs that inequality reflects
effort, which in turn reduces redistribution demand.
\end{abstract}

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} redistribution, relative performance, self-serving
attribution, fairness, online experiment\\
\noindent\textbf{JEL codes:} C91, D31, D63, D91

\section{Introduction}

A long-standing question in political economy is why individuals differ in
their preferences for redistribution.
One prominent explanation emphasises the role of \emph{beliefs about income
determinants}: people who believe that inequality arises from effort and talent
tend to favour less redistribution, while those who attribute it to luck or
circumstance tend to favour more
\citep{Piketty1995, Alesina2005, Benabou2006}.

A natural follow-up question is what shapes those beliefs.
Social comparisons are ubiquitous: workers learn their rank in a wage
distribution, students receive class rankings, and online platforms
continuously display relative performance metrics.
Does learning where one stands in a distribution affect how one interprets the
sources of inequality --- and through that channel, redistribution preferences?

This paper provides experimental evidence on this mechanism.
Participants complete a performance task and are randomly assigned to one of
three conditions: (i) \emph{no feedback} about relative standing; (ii) told
they performed \emph{above} a randomly matched peer (\emph{above} group);
or (iii) told they performed \emph{below} a matched peer (\emph{below}
group).
Subsequently, all participants act as impartial spectators and decide how to
distribute a fixed monetary sum between two workers, in two scenarios: one
where inequality arose from differential performance (merit), and one where it
arose from a lottery (luck).

The key findings are:
(1) Participants told they outperformed a peer attribute significantly more of
their own task outcome to effort (\(\overline{EL}_{\text{above}} = 7.6\))
compared to both the no-feedback (\(\overline{EL}_{\text{nofb}} = 6.8\)) and
below (\(\overline{EL}_{\text{below}} = 6.3\)) groups on a 0--10 scale.
(2) The same group redistributes less in the merit scenario
(\$1.87 vs.\ \$2.47 and \$2.39 in the other groups).
(3) The effect on redistribution is not statistically significant for the
below group relative to the no-feedback baseline, suggesting the response is
asymmetric.

These results are consistent with self-serving attribution
\citep{Hastorf1954,Miller1975}: individuals who learn they outperformed a peer
update upward their beliefs about the role of effort, and this updated belief
translates into lower redistribution in the merit scenario.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews the related literature.
Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the experimental design.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:lit}

This paper contributes to three strands of literature.

\paragraph{Beliefs about inequality and redistribution.}
A foundational insight from \citet{Piketty1995} is that beliefs about social
mobility and the role of effort versus luck determine preferences for
redistribution in equilibrium.
\citet{Alesina2005} provide empirical evidence: Americans attribute poverty
more to individual failure than Europeans, which partly explains the
cross-country gap in redistribution.
\citet{Benabou2006} model how self-serving motivated reasoning sustains
two equilibria --- a ``belief in a just world'' and a more luck-oriented view
--- with different steady-state redistribution levels.
Our experiment directly tests the mechanism by which an informational shock
(relative performance feedback) updates beliefs and behaviour.

\paragraph{Impartial spectator and fairness views.}
\citet{Cappelen2007} and \citet{Cappelen2013} use spectator designs to
classify subjects into fairness types (libertarian, meritocratic, egalitarian)
and show that a majority of individuals accept some redistribution away from
luck-generated inequality even when indifferent to merit-generated inequality.
We adapt their two-scenario design (merit and luck) to test whether
third-party redistribution decisions are affected by information about the
spectator's own relative performance --- a test of whether the spectator role
is truly impartial.

\paragraph{Self-serving attribution and social comparisons.}
Self-serving attribution refers to the tendency to attribute successes
internally (effort) and failures externally (luck or circumstance)
\citep{Miller1975, Mezulis2004}.
In an economic context, \citet{Babcock1995} document that parties in
bargaining inflate self-assessments of a fair outcome in a self-serving
direction.
Closer to our design, \citet{Deffains2016} show that individuals who
experience unfair treatment become more supportive of redistribution.
We add to this literature by focusing on the \emph{comparative} dimension:
the same absolute performance level can produce different attribution
depending on whether one learns one stands above or below a peer.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

The experiment was conducted online using JATOS and Prolific.
Participants completed two parts: a performance task (Part~1) and a
distribution task (Part~2).
The study took approximately 7--10 minutes and participants were compensated
according to standard Prolific rates plus a bonus determined by a randomly
selected distribution decision.

\subsection{Part 1: Code Recognition Task}

Participants completed a code-recognition task over five rounds of 45~seconds
each.
In each round, a target code was displayed at the top of the screen.
Participants were shown a grid of codes and had to check as many instances of
the target as possible.
Scoring: $+1$~point per correct identification, $-1$~point per false alarm,
$0$~points for a miss.
The maximum possible score was 200~points.

Before seeing their results, participants answered three pre-feedback
self-report questions:
(i) effort exerted (slider, 0--10);
(ii) guessed score (numeric, range $-200$ to $200$);
(iii) relative performance prediction (``I would perform better or the same''
vs.\ ``I would perform worse than the other participant'').

\subsection{Treatments: Relative Performance Feedback}

After the self-report, participants were randomly assigned to one of three
feedback conditions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No feedback.} Participants were simply informed they could
    proceed to Part~2.
  \item \textbf{Above.} Participants were shown their score and told:
    ``You performed above your randomly matched peer.''
  \item \textbf{Below.} Participants were shown their score and told:
    ``You performed below your randomly matched peer.''
\end{enumerate}

Matching was done using previously collected scores from a pilot wave,
ensuring that the ``above'' vs.\ ``below'' assignment was credible given each
participant's actual score.
Approximately half of the relative-feedback group was told they outperformed
(above) and half that they underperformed (below).

\subsection{Part 2: Distribution Decisions}

After the feedback screen, all participants acted as impartial spectators and
made two sequential redistribution decisions.

\subsubsection*{Decision 1 --- Merit Scenario}
Participants read:
\begin{quote}
  ``Two previous participants, Worker~A and Worker~B, completed the same task
  you just completed. \emph{[Worker A earned \$8; Worker B earned \$0 because
  they performed worse].}
  You must decide how to distribute a total of \$8 between them.''
\end{quote}
The distribution was presented as 9 discrete options ranging from \$0 to
Worker~B (keeping full inequality) to \$8 to Worker~B (reversing inequality),
with \$4 each as the equal split.
The outcome variable is the amount allocated to the lower earner, Worker~B
(\texttt{redist\_merit}, range \$0--\$8).

\subsubsection*{Decision 2 --- Luck Scenario}
After completing a demographics survey (including the effort--luck belief
slider), participants saw a second decision:
\begin{quote}
  ``Worker~C and Worker~D completed the same task. This time, earnings were
  assigned by a \textbf{lottery} with no regard for performance --- Worker~C
  won and received \$8; Worker~D received \$0.
  How would you reallocate the total of \$8?''
\end{quote}
The outcome variable is the amount allocated to Worker~D (\texttt{redist\_luck}).

\subsection{Key Outcome and Mediator Variables}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Redistribution (merit):} dollars allocated to Worker~B
    (\$0--\$8).
  \item \textbf{Redistribution (luck):} dollars allocated to Worker~D
    (\$0--\$8).
  \item \textbf{Effort--luck belief (\texttt{effort\_luck}):}
    a slider recorded after the merit decision: ``To what extent do you think
    the initial income of participants in the previous task was determined by
    effort versus luck?'' (0 = 100\% luck, 10 = 100\% effort).
    This variable serves as the hypothesised mediator.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sample}

The analysis sample consists of $N = 134$ participants after excluding
those with zero score and zero clicks (non-engagers) and those with
implausibly high scores ($>$\$5 allocated to Worker~B).
Table~\ref{tab:balance} presents descriptive statistics by treatment group.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Effort--Luck Beliefs}

Figure~\ref{fig:main} (left panel) shows mean effort--luck belief by treatment
group.
Participants told they outperformed a peer score markedly higher on the
effort-attribution scale ($\overline{EL} = 7.6$) relative to the no-feedback
group ($\overline{EL} = 6.8$) and the below group ($\overline{EL} = 6.3$).

Table~\ref{tab:regressions} (columns 1--2) presents OLS regressions of
\texttt{effort\_luck} on treatment dummies (baseline: no feedback), with and
without demographic controls.

approximately [XX] points (SE = [XX], $p$ = [XX]), while being told one
underperformed decreases it by approximately [XX] points, though this
coefficient is [not] statistically significant.
The pattern is consistent with self-serving attribution: winners attribute
the outcome to effort; losers do not systematically update in the opposite
direction.

\subsection{Redistribution in the Merit Scenario}

The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:main} shows mean redistribution in the
merit scenario.
The above group redistributes the least (\$1.87), compared to the
no-feedback baseline (\$2.47) and the below group (\$2.39).

Columns 3--4 of Table~\ref{tab:regressions} report OLS regressions of
\texttt{redist\_merit} on treatment dummies.
Being told one outperformed reduces redistribution by approximately [XX]
cents (SE = [XX], $p$ = [XX]) relative to the no-feedback group, corresponding
to a [XX]\% reduction from the no-feedback mean.
The below coefficient is small and statistically indistinguishable from zero,
confirming the asymmetry visible in Figure~\ref{fig:main}.
Results are robust to controlling for age, gender, and task score (column 4).

\subsection{Heterogeneity: Surprise}

To probe the mechanism further, we classify participants in the relative
group by whether the feedback was surprising.
\emph{Positively surprised} participants predicted they would perform worse
than or the same as their peer but were told they outperformed
(\texttt{above\_positively\_surprised} = 1).
\emph{Negatively surprised} participants predicted they would outperform
but were told they underperformed (\texttt{below\_negatively\_surprised} = 1).

If the self-serving attribution channel is operative, the redistribution
effect should be driven primarily by those who were unexpectedly told they
outperformed, as these individuals face the largest positive surprise.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper provides experimental evidence that relative performance feedback
shapes both beliefs about the role of effort in generating inequality and
downstream redistribution preferences.
Participants told they outperformed a peer attribute more of the task
outcome to effort and redistribute less in a merit scenario, consistent with
self-serving attribution updating meritocratic beliefs.
The effect is asymmetric: underperformers do not significantly increase
redistribution relative to a no-feedback baseline.

These findings have implications for the study of inequality and redistribution
in a world where performance comparisons are increasingly salient --- on
professional platforms, social networks, and in educational settings.
The self-serving updating documented here suggests that rising relative
visibility of performance rankings could systematically reduce support for
redistribution among those who ``win'' such comparisons, potentially
contributing to the polarisation of redistribution preferences.

Future work should (i) increase sample size to improve precision, particularly
for the heterogeneity analysis; (ii) test whether the effect persists when
participants are given their absolute score alongside relative feedback; and
(iii) examine whether the mediation through effort--luck beliefs is statistically
confirmed with a formal mediation analysis.
